**TL;DR:** A robotic warehouse should not stop because one robot does. To keep operating safely, its controller must do three things after a failure: establish the robot's physical state, contain the uncertain area, and escalate recovery only as far as necessary.

## The warehouse

[Volume DIVE](https://volume.eu/en/warehouse-automation-company/container-warehouse/) is a compact container-storage system in which the storage rack is also the robots' road network. Rails form a grid around stacks of containers on several levels. Small mobile robots, called **Snappers**, travel over the grid and move containers between stacks and operator ports.

Each robot can drive in two rail directions, rotate its gripper tower, and extend the gripper beyond its body. Several robots share the same dense grid, so a machine that stops or moves unexpectedly becomes a physical obstacle for all nearby traffic.

A robot accepts a move, enters an intersection, and stops reporting progress. The controller cannot simply mark its task as failed. The robot still occupies physical space, may still hold a container, and may now block the routes of several other robots.

This is the central recovery problem: the controller's record says what should have happened, while safety depends on what physically happened. Closing that gap requires three steps:

1. Establish the robot's actual state.
2. Contain the area whose safety is uncertain.
3. Escalate until the system reaches a trustworthy state again.

## 1. Establish the physical state

In a software-only system, retrying a failed operation may be harmless. A robot command changes the physical world. Retrying it without knowing what already happened can make the situation worse.

We encountered failures at every boundary between software and hardware:

- **Motion:** a robot stopped between cells, ignored a command, or could no longer steer accurately because a clutch had loosened or a belt had stretched.
- **Mechanics:** wires broke, motors overheated, batteries died, and containers became damaged or sat unevenly in a stack.
- **State and identity:** an operator scanned the wrong container, a robot held a different container from the one the controller expected, or an unregistered test robot appeared on the grid.
- **Communication:** a connection disappeared while the controller still needed to determine whether the robot had stopped.
- **Environment:** loose hardware landed on the rails or someone pressed an emergency-stop button.

The error message alone is not enough. A disconnected robot may be stationary or still moving. A stopped robot may be safely inside a cell or stranded between two cells. It may also be holding a container with its gripper extended. Those states require different recovery actions.

Before acting, the controller reconciles its intended state with the available observations: position, alarms, connectivity, wheel orientation, gripper position, and the identity of any container being carried. It must recover from the state the hardware reached, not the state it hoped to reach.

Not every delay is a failure. At a port, an operator may still be working with a container. A known delay can continue; a lost message, incorrect container, or abandoned operation creates an unknown state that must first be reconciled.

## 2. Contain the uncertainty

Robots share a dense rail grid. If one stops in an aisle, its failure changes the safe operating area for every other robot on that level. Other robots may already be moving toward space that is no longer available.

The controller therefore blocks the uncertain robot's physical area before attempting recovery. Other robots can continue outside that area when a safe route remains available. This shrinks the blast radius: one broken robot does not automatically stop the whole warehouse.

This becomes especially important when a robot takes longer than expected. Space must not be released merely because its planned reservation expired. The controller must confirm that the robot has progressed far enough; otherwise the occupied area remains blocked.

## 3. Escalate only as far as necessary

Stopping the entire warehouse and asking an operator to fix every problem sounds safe, but it is not an operational strategy. Development robots fail frequently, and early production hardware still fails often enough that the warehouse must degrade gracefully.

We used an escalation ladder:

1. **Recover automatically.** Retract the gripper, return a robot from an in-between position to a known cell, or realign its wheels when the controller has enough trustworthy state to do so safely.
2. **Isolate the robot.** Block the time and space around it while other robots continue elsewhere.
3. **Evacuate it.** Move the robot to a service station if it can still travel safely.
4. **Block the level.** Stop all traffic on the affected level when the failure cannot be contained locally.
5. **Stop the warehouse.** Escalate to a full stop when the controller can no longer establish a safe operating area.

The controller advances only when it knows enough to avoid increasing the uncertainty. Otherwise it moves down the ladder and trades more availability for a larger safety boundary.

## Resilience is controlled degradation

A resilient warehouse does not pretend that failures are avoidable or harmless. It makes each failure move in one direction: from an unknown physical state toward a known one.

First establish what happened. Then isolate only what may be unsafe. Finally, widen that boundary—from one robot, to one level, to the entire warehouse—only when the available evidence cannot justify continued operation. The result is not uninterrupted operation at any cost; it is the largest part of the warehouse that can keep working safely.
